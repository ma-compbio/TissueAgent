# Finalized Hypotheses for TNBC Spatial Dataset

## H1: Invasive cancer spots at the tumor–lymphocyte interface show a higher basal-like epithelial program (KRT14/KRT5) relative to the luminal-like program (EPCAM/KRT8/KRT18) compared with invasive cancer spots located in the tumor core without lymphocyte neighbors.

This hypothesis focuses on how invasive triple-negative breast cancer (TNBC) cells at the "Invasive cancer + lymphocytes" interface differ from tumor cores in their epithelial programs. In this dataset, spots classified as invasive cancer or mixed "Invasive cancer + lymphocytes" can be characterized by basal keratin markers (such as KRT14/KRT5) versus luminal keratin and epithelial markers (such as EPCAM/KRT8/KRT18). The hypothesis proposes that interface spots where tumor cells neighbor lymphocyte-rich regions show a bias toward basal-like programs relative to more luminal-weighted cores, consistent with observations of mixed tumor–immune regions and keratin heterogeneity (supported by OBSERVATION 4, OBSERVATION 6, OBSERVATION 9).

Analytically, spots are grouped into interface versus core tumor regions based on a k-nearest-neighbor graph on the Visium spatial coordinates, focusing on invasive cancer and "Invasive cancer + lymphocytes" classifications. For each spot, basal and luminal keratin modules are scored, and the balance of these programs is compared between interface and core groups. A pattern in which interface spots consistently show higher basal-like and lower luminal-like module scores than cores would support the hypothesis; similar or opposite patterns between groups would argue against it. Spatial neighbors are defined using a 6-nearest-neighbor graph on the Visium spatial coordinates. Each comparison group must contain at least 20 spots; if a group is too small, closely related classes are merged or the contrast is simplified.

## H2: Lymphocyte-classified spots adjacent to invasive cancer (tumor-proximal aggregates) are relatively enriched for T cell markers compared to B cell markers, whereas lymphocyte spots embedded in stromal/normal territories are less T cell–biased or more B cell–balanced.

This hypothesis examines whether lymphocytes that are spatially proximal to invasive tumor spots differ from lymphocytes embedded in stromal or more normal-appearing neighborhoods. In the classification, spots labeled "Lymphocytes" or combined lymphocyte-containing labels (such as "Normal + stroma + lymphocytes") can be contrasted based on whether their nearest neighbors are invasive cancer versus stroma or normal tissue. The key question is whether tumor-proximal lymphocyte spots are biased toward T-cell marker programs (CD3D/CD3E/CD2) compared with B-cell marker programs (MS4A1/CD79A), reflecting a more T cell–dominated infiltrate at the tumor margin (supported by OBSERVATION 4, OBSERVATION 5, OBSERVATION 7, OBSERVATION 9).

For this hypothesis, lymphocyte-containing spots are first identified and then partitioned by whether their nearest spatial neighbors (defined by a k-nearest-neighbor graph) are invasive cancer versus stromal or normal classifications. T-cell and B-cell marker modules are computed per spot and compared between tumor-proximal and stromal/normal-proximal lymphocyte groups. Enrichment of T-cell markers and relative depletion of B-cell markers near tumor would support the hypothesis, whereas a lack of bias or a B-cell–dominant signal at the tumor edge would refute it. Spatial neighbors are defined using a 6-nearest-neighbor graph on the Visium spatial coordinates. Each comparison group must contain at least 20 spots; if a group is too small, closely related classes are merged or the contrast is simplified.

## H3: Spots at tumor–stroma interfaces are enriched for myeloid marker expression (LYZ, CD68) relative to both tumor-core and stroma-core spots, indicating preferential localization of myeloid cells at invasive fronts.

This hypothesis addresses myeloid-cell–like activity at interfaces between invasive TNBC and surrounding stroma. Spots annotated as "Invasive cancer + lymphocytes", "Stroma", and related stromal classifications (including "Stroma + adipose tissue" and "Normal + stroma + lymphocytes") can be stratified into tumor–stroma interface versus tumor-core and stroma-core locations using spatial neighbors. The hypothesis predicts that myeloid marker programs—captured by genes such as LYZ and CD68—are most enriched at tumor–stroma interfaces compared with either tumor-core or stroma-core spots, in line with observations of immune-rich borders and mixed tumor–immune regions (supported by OBSERVATION 2, OBSERVATION 5, OBSERVATION 8, OBSERVATION 9).

Here, invasive cancer and stromal spots are used to define tumor-core, stroma-core, and tumor–stroma interface regions using the spatial neighbor graph. Myeloid marker modules based on LYZ, CD68 and related genes are scored across these groups. If myeloid module scores peak in the interface group compared with both tumor-core and stroma-core spots, the hypothesis is supported; if myeloid signals are uniform or higher in cores rather than interfaces, it is refuted. Spatial neighbors are defined using a 6-nearest-neighbor graph on the Visium spatial coordinates. Each comparison group must contain at least 20 spots; if a group is too small, closely related classes are merged or the contrast is simplified.

## H4: The spatial adjacency between lymphocyte-classified spots and invasive cancer regions is stronger than expected from shared localization to high-quality (high nCount_RNA / nFeature_RNA) tissue areas, i.e., the lymphocyte–tumor neighbor association remains significant after adjusting for QC gradients.

This hypothesis tests whether the apparent enrichment of lymphocyte–tumor adjacency is a robust spatial feature rather than an artifact of technical variation. Using spots labeled as "Lymphocytes" or lymphocyte-containing mixed classes (for example, "Invasive cancer + lymphocytes" and "Normal + stroma + lymphocytes"), the analysis models whether a spot is lymphocyte-rich as a function of its spatial adjacency to invasive cancer, while adjusting for quality-control metrics such as nCount_RNA and nFeature_RNA if needed. The goal is to assess whether lymphocyte enrichment at tumor borders persists after accounting for sequencing depth and complexity (supported by OBSERVATION 3, OBSERVATION 4, OBSERVATION 9).

The planned analysis uses a logistic regression framework where the outcome is whether a spot is lymphocyte-rich, and key predictors include adjacency to invasive cancer as defined by the k-nearest-neighbor graph. Quality-control metrics such as nCount_RNA and nFeature_RNA can be included as covariates or explored in sensitivity analyses. A robust, positive association between tumor adjacency and lymphocyte status after adjusting for QC covariates would support the hypothesis; if this association disappears or reverses once QC is considered, the hypothesis would be considered refuted. Spatial neighbors are defined using a 6-nearest-neighbor graph on the Visium spatial coordinates. Each comparison group must contain at least 20 spots; if a group is too small, closely related classes are merged or the contrast is simplified.
