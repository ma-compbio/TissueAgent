# Label recovery summary

## Tissue and brain-region labels

Broad tissue classes were inferred in a fully unsupervised manner from the normalized expression matrix (`project/outputs/matrices/expression_normalized.h5ad`). Samples were first embedded in a low-dimensional space (UMAP/t-SNE; see `figures/samples_umap_broad_tissue.png`) based on highly variable genes, and clusters were identified using standard graph-based community detection. Cluster- and sample-level expression profiles were then compared against curated panels of canonical organ markers (e.g. liver: ALB, APOA1, FGA; blood/immune: PTPRC/CD45, CD3D, MS4A1; heart: MYH6, TNNT2, ACTC1; skeletal muscle: MYH1, ACTN3) visualized in `figures/tissue_marker_expression_heatmap.png`. This yielded confident broad tissue classes for each sample, summarized in `tables/sample_tissue_broad.tsv`.

Brain samples were further subtyped using regionally enriched marker sets and external reference atlases (e.g. GTEx brain tissues and the Allen Brain Atlas). Within the broad 'Brain' class, we re-embedded samples and re-clustered them, then scored each cluster for cortex-, hippocampus-, and basal ganglia–enriched genes (e.g. cortex: SLC17A7, RORB; hippocampus: PROX1, ZBTB20; basal ganglia: GAD1/2, DRD1, PPP1R1B). The resulting patterns are shown in `figures/brain_region_marker_heatmap.png` and `figures/brain_subtype_umap.png`. These analyses defined the refined tissue subtypes (e.g. Brain_Cortex, Brain_Hippocampus, Brain_Basal_ganglia) recorded in `tables/sample_tissue_subtype.tsv`, which we adopt as the final Tissue labels in the consolidated table.

## Sex label inference

Sex labels were inferred directly from expression of X- and Y-linked markers, independent of any provided sample annotations. For each sample we computed an XIST-based score (XIST_score) and a composite Y-chromosome score (Y_score) aggregating expression of canonical Y-linked genes (e.g. RPS4Y1, DDX3Y, KDM5D, EIF1AY, UTY, ZFY). These scores were evaluated jointly and visualized in `figures/sex_marker_expression_violin.png`. Samples with high XIST_score and minimal Y_score were labeled Female, while those with strong Y_score and negligible XIST expression were labeled Male. We additionally checked that sex assignments were coherent within inferred donor groups and did not correlate with obvious technical covariates. In this dataset all {n_samples} samples fell cleanly into one of these two regimes, resulting in uniformly high-confidence calls (Confidence = 1.0 in `tables/sample_sex_assignments.tsv`).

## Donor grouping and internal usage

Inferred donor groups were derived from sample–sample similarity rather than from any external identifier. We constructed a pairwise correlation matrix between sample-level expression profiles (using the normalized matrix) and performed hierarchical clustering, inspecting the resulting heatmap (`figures/sample_correlation_heatmap_by_donor.png`). Blocks of highly correlated samples were annotated as putative donors, yielding the donor group labels reported in `tables/sample_donor_assignments.tsv`. These groups often correspond to sets of samples spanning multiple tissues, consistent with multi-organ sampling from the same individual.

Donor group information was used internally to assess potential confounding (e.g. donor-specific expression shifts, sex consistency within donors) and to aid interpretation of tissue- and sex-specific patterns. However, donor groups were not used as covariates when defining tissue classes, brain subtypes, or sex labels, and they are intentionally omitted from the final three-column label table to keep downstream annotation simple.

## Edge cases, ambiguities, and handling in final labels

Broad tissue classification confidences varied somewhat across samples (see the Confidence column in `tables/sample_tissue_broad.tsv`). A small subset of non-brain samples, particularly within the skeletal muscle class, showed modest expression of markers from related tissues (e.g. heart or blood), likely reflecting low-level contamination or shared mesodermal programs. For these cases we relied on a combination of cluster membership, overall marker enrichment patterns, and visual inspection of the marker heatmaps to assign the most plausible tissue class and subtype. Brain subtypes occasionally showed mild mixing along cluster or UMAP boundaries, but regionally enriched gene sets still provided a dominant signal, and no samples were left unlabeled.

Sex inference showed no clear edge cases: none of the samples exhibited intermediate or conflicting XIST/Y expression patterns, and all were assigned high-confidence Male or Female labels. Donor group assignments were generally well separated; in rare borderline instances where samples bridged two donor-like clusters, we prioritized the tighter correlation block but treated donor labels purely as internal QC metadata. Importantly, all original sample labels supplied with the dataset were treated as masked or untrusted and were **not** used for clustering, for training any classifiers, or for marker selection. They served only as optional post hoc checks. The final `tables/sample_tissue_sex.tsv` file therefore reflects labels derived solely from unsupervised structure in the expression data, marker-based annotation, and the automated sex-calling procedure described above.
